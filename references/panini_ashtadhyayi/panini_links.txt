Pāṇini’s Aṣṭādhyāyī (composed around the 5th to 4th century BCE) functions as an algorithmic and mathematical engine for language rather than a traditional descriptive book. It treats grammar like a formal system of equations, variables, and logical operations.
Core Mechanisms of the "Arithmetic of Grammar"
• Approximately 4,000 Sūtras (Rules): Pāṇini compressed the entire morphology, phonology, and syntax of Sanskrit into about 3,983 to 4,000 aphoristic rules. These act like program functions or mathematical formulas.
• Metalanguage and Variables (Śiva Sūtras): He grouped sounds into shorthand sound clusters using the Śiva Sūtras, which function identically to variables or arrays in computer code.
• Anubandhas (Data Tagging): He attached silent, non-pronounced marker letters (anubandhas) to roots and suffixes. These tags do not appear in the final spoken word, but they dictate how the root behaves mathematically during transformations (like a hidden data attribute).
• Adhikāra (Rule Inheritance / Scope): Rules in the Aṣṭādhyāyī cascade downwards (adhikāra) from a parent rule to all subsequent child rules until a boundary condition or exception stops them—mirroring object-oriented class inheritance.
• Paribhāṣā (Meta-Rules & Conflict Resolution): When two or more grammatical rules compete or conflict for the same word construction, Pāṇini built a logical priority and exception system (such as the famous resolution on right-hand side vs. hierarchical rule application) to ensure zero ambiguity.
Why It Resembles Computation
Modern linguists and computer scientists (including Noam Chomsky and early computational lexicographers) have likened the Aṣṭādhyāyī to a Turing machine or a generative grammar engine. Instead of just listing words, it acts as a "root-and-suffix language machine": you input a root and a set of grammatical operators, run the rules sequentially, and it deterministically outputs a grammatically valid Sanskrit word or sentence.
